Who leaves, and may a model say who?

Client: A shared-services organisation of 4,000 employees in Italy, Poland, Germany and Spain (synthetic), whose HR team wants a retention model and has to decide whether it may use one.

Who leaves and when, can a model warn in time, is it fair, and what may HR do with it under the AI Act?

What I did

  • Fitted an attrition model on one quarter and scored it on the next two, which it had never seen, against a checklist of the same signs and the data's own ceiling.
  • Audited it by sex, age, country and working time, and wrote its model card: the provider's answers to the 14 questions the AI Act register asks of a high-risk system.
  • Measured how long people stay with survival analysis on tenure, counting people hired before the data began from the tenure they had then.
  • Delivered a browser tool for the client's own HRIS export, teams never names, and a workbook reconciled with Python.

What it found

  • Most of the value is in knowing which signs to watch: the weights add 0.03 of AUC over counting them, and the model closes 84% of the gap between chance and the ceiling.
  • Country of work is not an input, so Germany is over-read, 3.6% predicted where 2.2% resign. Fixing it is a significant change under the AI Act, so the provider's duties come first.
  • People paid below band stay 22.4 of their first 36 months against 31.3, the same effect in year one as after it.

With real data

  • Inputs as they stood on each scoring date, with pay position changing at every raise.
  • A decision on the engagement input: an individual survey score breaks the confidentiality most surveys promise.

How it is checked. Statistics written from scratch and tested against closed forms (AUC as Mann-Whitney, the Cox score test as the log-rank test); the tool agrees with Python on 1,530 values, the workbook on 121, recalculated by LibreOffice in CI.
