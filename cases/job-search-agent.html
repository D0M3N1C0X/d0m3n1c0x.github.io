Which postings deserve an application?

Client: A job seeker in HR applying across Europe, with more postings than evenings. The figures are measured on an invented candidate, so they can be published.

Which of this week's postings deserve an application, and how far can a scorer that decides it be trusted?

What I did

  • Pulled postings from the public APIs of eight applicant tracking systems and two job feeds, each source tested offline on a captured answer.
  • Scored every posting against each positioning track with rules a person can read: hard gates first (a language actually required, years asked, location, seniority, terms), then keywords, every point explained.
  • Wrote 160 postings labelled apply, maybe or skip for the candidate, and kept 30 aside, written after the last change to the rules, to measure each version on postings it was not tuned on.
  • Delivered a dashboard, a CV and cover letter that may only cite the profile, and a one-line install.

What it found

  • The first rules scored half the held-out postings as a person would: 15 of 30. Rules written against labelled postings raised that to 20.
  • The next version fixed every miss of its round and still scored 21 of 30 on a new held-out set, like the one before: phrase-by-phrase rules had stopped generalising. The report says so, and quotes that figure, not the 115 of 130 on the postings it was tuned on.
  • The errors left are the cheap kind: three skips scored as worth applying, nothing worth applying to thrown away. Whether a language is required is the hardest rule: right on 3 of 5.

With real data

  • The candidate's own decisions as labels: what was sent against what was skipped, compared from 20 decisions on.
  • A week of real postings labelled blind, to measure the market instead of written cases.

How it is checked. 380 offline tests; the benchmark runs at every change with a floor on the tuning set, so a rule that makes the scorer worse fails the build; the held-out set is replaced before the next figures are published.
